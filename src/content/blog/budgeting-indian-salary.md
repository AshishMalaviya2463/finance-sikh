The 50/30/20 rule can help to quickly allocate your post-tax income to three main areas. However, many Indians will not exactly match the percentage breakdown as rent, family support, school fees, health, travel and loan repayments can take up a lot of your income. The benefit of the rule is that you are ensuring that you are allocating every rupee towards something.

A typical breakdown would be to allocate 50% of your income towards your needs, 30% to your wants and 20% to savings or paying off extra debt. It is important to understand what falls in each category. For example, your home loan EMI may be a need whilst a subscription may be a want. Family support can be a must for some people whereas others may be able to give more flexibility to this category. Only once you have made your categories relevant to your situation will the rule be beneficial.

## Take your post-tax income

Always use your post-tax income (what you receive in your bank account after payroll deductions). If your post-tax income is Rs60,000 per month then you would allocate Rs30,000 to your needs, Rs18,000 to your wants and Rs12,000 to your savings or paying extra debt. This is just an example to demonstrate how the breakdown works.

If your income is variable due to incentives, overtime or commissions then it is best to create a budget based on your fixed income. Any variable income can be placed in a separate category until received.

## What should I assign to each category?

Needs – Essential expenses that you must pay to survive. This can include rent, food, electricity, basic travel, essential medication, minimum debt repayments and essential insurance. Wants – Non-essential expenses such as eating out, entertainment, upgrades, hobbies and non-essentials. Savings – Can include creating an emergency fund, long-term investments and paying extra debt.

Some things may fall into both categories. Whilst a mobile phone plan is essential for work, you may not need the most expensive plan. A car may be essential for travel but any extras/ upgrades can be a want. Family support may be a must so this shouldn’t be categorised as a want. Try to write why you are spending on each major thing.

## Example of an Indian salary

Let’s say your post-tax income is Rs60,000. You pay rent of Rs16,000, food and essentials of Rs7,000, utility and phone bills of Rs2,500, travel of Rs3,000, family support of Rs8,000 and EMI of Rs9,000. This totals up to Rs45,500. This means that you wouldn’t be able to follow the classic 50/30/20 rule as you would need to adjust your assumptions or categories. The correct answer here would be not to try to trick yourself into thinking your family support is a want or that you don’t need to pay your EMI.

You should first separate your must pay from your adjustable. Your rent could be fixed until the end of the lease, you could reduce your travel costs by travelling differently and subscriptions can easily be cancelled. You could also have a temporary breakdown of your percentages and review this when you get a raise, pay off your loan or move somewhere. A budget is there to help you make decisions.

## Budget for annual costs

Insurance, school fees, festival travel, car servicing and appliance replacements can cause a balanced monthly budget to go out of balance. They won’t necessarily be emergencies but you can still budget for them. Try to work out roughly how much you will pay yearly and divide by 12. For example if you have an annual bill of Rs24,000 then you should be setting aside Rs2,000 per month.

This will help to keep your cashflow in check. Otherwise you may feel that you have extra money for 11 months and then need to use your credit card for your big bill. You should also keep your emergency savings separate.

## Savings and Debt

The 20% can often be seen as savings but you may have many needs. You may need to create an emergency fund, save for an upcoming goal, invest for the long-term and pay off your expensive debt. This will depend on the interest rates, liquidity, risk and your situation. A budget rule will not determine which debt or investment you should prioritise.

If you have a minimum EMI payment then it is best to always allocate them to the same category. If your rule considers them to be needs then you can track your extra principal payments in the savings/debt section. Just ensure you aren’t double counting. Your spreadsheet should show what you are actually paying.

## What if my rent takes up half my salary?

Large city’s can mean that rent and travel can take up a lot of your income. If you are providing for your family then you may also find that you cannot fit the textbook breakdown. You could have a more realistic breakdown by increasing your needs and decreasing your wants. This is to show what you are spending your money on. You can always work on improving this gradually.

Some costs can only be negotiated when your contract ends. You may need to make a longer term decision such as moving closer to work or sharing a house. This will have its own trade offs but your budget should highlight these decisions.

## Monthly review

At the end of each month you should review what you were planning to spend and what you actually spent. Only look at the categories that had a significant impact on your total. If your food was higher due to family visiting then note this. If you keep going over your budget when shopping online then set a limit or think about putting a cooling off period in place.

A budget should be reviewed if there is a change in your life. Whether it be a new job, child, moving home, new loan or family support. You don’t need to review it weekly but having a quick monthly review can help you to see if you are drifting.

## Spreadsheet or Budget App

Many households will be fine using a spreadsheet. Create columns for date, amount, category and essential/want/variable. Some may prefer to use a budget app but ensure that you know the privacy settings and how it categorises your expenses. It should make your life easier.

Do not use your budget as an excuse to put off essential medical costs. If you are unable to afford your essentials then this may be an issue with your structure. Rather than focusing on your percentages you should look to understand your costs and get the right support.

## Conclusion

The 50/30/20 rule can be a helpful guide but many Indian household budgets will require a different breakdown. Start with your post tax income, ensure you are categorising honestly, add in your family support and annual costs and track your debt repayments. Don’t double count and create a budget that reflects your responsibilities and allows you to make informed decisions.

## Consider your values

Some households will have family support, variable medical costs or contribute to a family event that isn’t essential but is important. A strict spreadsheet may categorise these as wants but they are a must. Your budget should reflect your situation. You could create a family support line to show that you are spending on this.

Your budget can also show you what is a must pay and what is a future adjustable cost. You may not be able to negotiate your lease but you could cancel your annual subscription today. This will help to ensure that your plan doesn’t assume everything is adjustable. Look at what is adjustable first. However if you are already paying more than you earn on essentials then this won’t help.

## Create a variable income budget

If you have freelance income, commissions or overtime then try to create a budget based on a conservative estimate of your fixed income. Use your extra income to cover your variable costs, savings or goals. Don’t consider large invoices as extra to spend. If your income is less than usual you can refer to your defined priorities to ensure you are keeping your home, food, essential travel and required repayments.

You can still use the classic percentages but having a monthly cashflow calendar can be more useful. This will help you to see when your income is coming in and when your bills are due. This can cause issues with your budget even if you have enough annual income.

## Example monthly review

A useful review would be to ask yourself a few questions. Did you go over your budget? Was it a one off or regular issue? Will you need to make changes next month? If your food was higher due to price increases then you may need to update your estimate. If you are frequently ordering food online then you may want to set a limit. If your family support increased due to medical needs then it could be a genuine new must.

Try to keep your review factual. You don’t want to be labelling everything as good or bad. A monthly budget should allow you to enjoy normal things where possible. If your budget is too strict then you are unlikely to find it useful. Look at your numbers and make 1 or 2 realistic changes. See if these helped next month.
