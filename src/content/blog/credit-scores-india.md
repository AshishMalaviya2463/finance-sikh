Your credit score is calculated using a credit scoring algorithm and information held by the credit information company. Lenders can use this alongside the credit report, income, obligations and other information to help them decide whether to lend you.

In India, credit information companies will hold reports based on information provided by credit institutions (credit institutions are members). Credit scoring algorithms can vary between companies as they are proprietary. As noted by the RBI, there is no standard scoring formula. Be wary of sites that claim to know exactly how much your score will go up by for doing one thing.

## What’s on a Credit Report?

A credit report can include lots of different pieces of information like your identity, credit accounts, repayment history, outstanding balances, credit limits and credit inquiries. This will vary depending on the report and the information provided by credit institutions. You should look at the names, addresses and account details provided. Whilst having the correct score is important, having the right account details and repayment status is also important.

Check the date of the report and the date the lender last reported. It can take some time for the latest payment to show on your report if they haven’t reported within their cycle. The RBI have given guidance on how often they should report. Check the current guidance. It doesn’t necessarily mean they’ve ignored your payment.

## Repayment History & Utilisation

Repayment history can impact how lenders see you. If you’re missing payments on a loan or card, this may be reported and can show on your report. However, if you’re making on time payments, this will help ensure your repayment history is accurate. However, there are other factors to consider such as other information, scoring algorithms and lender criteria.

Utilisation usually refers to how much of your available revolving credit you’re using. Having a high balance on your card may be seen differently than a low balance. There isn’t one specific percentage that will guarantee you a score. The balance and date can be important. Your card issuer may not have the same statement date as you. Don’t be tempted to use social media ‘magic numbers’.

## Credit Inquiries & Applications

If the lender are looking at your report as part of an application, they may show as an inquiry. If there are multiple applications within a short space of time, this can be seen differently than if you occasionally apply. If you’re checking your own report via an authorised consumer portal, this won’t count the same as multiple applications. Check with the credit information company for more details.

Don’t apply for multiple products to see which one will approve you. This can cause extra admin for the lender. If you’re rejected for a loan, check why, ensure there are no mistakes on your report and understand whether you could afford to borrow. Apply elsewhere.

## How to view your report

Go to the official website of each credit information company (CIC) that holds your credit history and complete its identity-verification process. RBI directions require each CIC to provide an individual, on request and after due authentication, one free full credit report (FFCR), including the credit score, once in each calendar year. The report is electronic, and each CIC must publish the process for obtaining it on its website. This is a separate entitlement with each CIC; it is not a single report shared across all bureaus.

If you find an error, raise a dispute with the CIC and the lender or other credit institution that supplied the information. Keep the complaint reference and supporting records. RBI’s credit-information framework provides a compensation mechanism for qualifying delays in correction, subject to the prescribed conditions. If the complaint remains unresolved for more than 30 days, or you are dissatisfied with the response, you may use the RBI Complaint Management System where the matter is covered by the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme. Check the current eligibility and filing instructions before escalating. Ensure all your accounts match what you have. Check there isn’t an outstanding loan, a late payment when you’ve paid on time, duplicate or not your account. Ensure your personal details are correct. Keep copies of statements, closure letters, payment confirmation and any other documents to help with disputes.

## Disputing Mistakes

If there seems to be a mistake on your account or repayment, use the official dispute process with the credit information company. If applicable, dispute with the lender providing the information too. Be specific about which account, what you’re disputing and what you want changed. Include any evidence to back up your dispute. Don’t send any sensitive documents through unofficial links.

Keep a note of your complaint reference. The bureau may need the reporting institution to investigate as they can’t always change the data. If you’re still having issues, check current RBI complaint process and how to escalate. Ensure you’re using the correct process.

## Good Habits

Set reminders or automatic payments so you don’t miss any due dates. Ensure you have enough funds in the account to make the payments. Check your statements to ensure there are no unexpected charges. Ensure any facilities you’ve closed are shown as such. Only borrow if you’ve considered whether you can fully afford to repay (including interest, fees and multiple EMIs).

Only close older accounts if you’ve considered the terms of the account and your needs. Don’t use one loan to pay off another without understanding the overall cost. You won’t improve your score by borrowing unnecessarily.

## Identity Theft

If there’s an unknown account or inquiry, this could be an indicator there’s an issue. Contact the lender and credit information company using official channels. Secure your bank and email accounts. Follow their guidance. Don’t ignore any suspicious entries just because the balance is low. Keep a note of your complaint. Check your report has been amended.

Be wary of services offering to remove accurate negative information or guarantee a certain number of points. Credit reports are governed by strict data processes. You can’t legally turn accurate history into a better score. They shouldn’t ask for your account passwords.

## Misconceptions

Having a high score doesn’t guarantee you’ll get approved for all loans. Having a low score doesn’t mean there’ll be one reason you’re being rejected. Scoring algorithms aren’t the same between companies and there isn’t a guaranteed number of points for doing one thing. Checking your report isn’t the same as applying for new credit. This will help reduce any unnecessary stress.

## Conclusion

Your credit score should be viewed alongside your full credit report and the lenders overall decision. Make sure you’re making your payments on time, ensure the information is correct and use official channels to amend any mistakes. Don’t borrow just to chase a number. It can take time for changes to happen and depend on the reporting and scoring process. Don’t promise a specific increase. Use current guidance from the RBI and credit information companies.

## What to do if you’re rejected

There can be many reasons for you being rejected. This can include your income, current obligations, how much you’re asking for, lender policies and the information on your credit report. Don’t assume it’s because of your score. Check why you were rejected, ensure there are no mistakes on your report and calculate whether you could afford to repay. Applying multiple times without understanding why can cause more inquiries.

If your account is showing as overdue due to a genuine mistake, use the official dispute process. If the information is correct, focus on getting your obligations up to date and ensuring you’re making sustainable payments. Don’t pay to guarantee it’ll be removed. Accurate data will be dealt with using the correct reporting and dispute process.

## Avoid falling for scams

Never give anyone claiming to fix your credit score your OTP, banking password or permission to access remotely. Ensure the website address and customer care details are correct. Be wary of guaranteed increases, fake government clearance certificates and advance fees to remove accurate history. Any legitimate dispute should be made through the credit information company and lender.

## Keep borrowing affordable

You can have a clean credit report but take on more debt than you can afford within your household budget. Before agreeing to a loan, calculate whether the EMI would fit within your rent, food, utilities, insurance, supporting family and repayments. Don’t forget to include irregular costs. Just because a lender is willing to give you a limit, doesn’t mean you can afford to borrow the full limit.

If you’re struggling to make repayments, contact the lender using official channels. Don’t ignore any notices. Ask what options you have available to you. Don’t take out a high cost loan to cover a regular EMI without calculating your new overall repayment. Your credit score is a record based metric. You should have a budget in place for your household.

## Checking your report

Once your dispute has been resolved, obtain a new report. Allow time for the relevant updates to be reported. The score won’t necessarily increase by a set amount. Even if there’s a mistake, your score can depend on other information and the proprietary algorithm. The aim of disputing is to have accurate data.

## Read the report before trying to change the number

A score is a summary, not a diagnosis. Two people can have the same score for different reasons, so the useful work begins with the account-level information in the credit report. Check whether each loan is yours, whether the account is open or closed, whether the outstanding balance looks right and whether the payment history matches your records. Look carefully at dates: a payment made near a reporting cut-off may not appear in the same month you expect.

If you find a mismatch, keep the relevant statement, payment receipt, closure letter or lender email. Raise a dispute with the credit information company and, where appropriate, the lender that supplied the data. Write a clear explanation and identify the account and month involved. Save the complaint reference and response. Do not pay an agent who promises to erase accurate negative information or produce a particular score. A legitimate correction process is not a guaranteed score-improvement service.

## Why a closed loan may still appear

A credit report can retain historical account information after a loan has been repaid. Seeing a closed account is not automatically an error. What matters is whether its status, balance and dates are accurate. If the report still shows an amount due after you have a no-dues certificate, ask the lender to investigate and update the information sent to the bureau. Keep the certificate even after the account disappears from your active list.

## Avoid turning score improvement into more borrowing

It is easy to hear that a “credit mix” is helpful and conclude that you should take a loan you do not need. That is a poor reason to borrow. Interest, fees and the risk of missing a payment are real; a possible score effect is uncertain and depends on the wider report. Use credit only when there is a genuine need and the repayment fits your budget. A good score cannot make an unaffordable EMI safe.

The most useful outcome is an accurate report and borrowing history you can comfortably maintain. The score may respond over time, but lenders still assess income, existing obligations, stability and their own policy. Treat the number as one part of the picture, not a promise of approval.
