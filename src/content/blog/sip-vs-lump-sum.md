A Systematic Investment Plan and Lump Sum are similar, however the main difference is the method in which you invest. A Systematic Investment Plan will invest your money into your chosen scheme by making scheduled regular contributions. With a Lump Sum you invest a larger amount of money at once (subject to processing and scheme rules). Neither name of the investment will tell you if the scheme you are investing into is suitable and both options do not guarantee that you will profit.

This question often comes up when you have some bonus money, maturity money, inheritance or savings to invest. Perhaps you want to invest some money now but are worried that the market will drop shortly, or maybe you only have a small amount of money to invest each salary. You should consider where the money is coming from and why you are looking to invest now.

## How they work

With a Systematic Investment Plan you are investing a set amount into your chosen scheme on a regular basis. You will buy units at the relevant NAV on each day your investment goes through. You will be investing the same amount each time, however depending on the NAV, you will receive a different amount of units. The schedule can help to avoid having to decide each month, however this will depend on your bank and scheme rules.

With a Lump Sum you will invest the larger amount of money you have available. If the scheme performs well then you will benefit from the increase as it will be invested as a whole. On the flip side, if the scheme performs poorly you will lose the whole amount invested. You can see when you have invested the money. You may want to invest your money in a Lump Sum to avoid having unused money, however this depends on market performance and the asset you are investing into.

## Timing Risk vs Investment Risk

Timing Risk relates to your chosen point of investment. Investment Risk relates to the assets within the scheme not performing as expected or losing value. Although a Systematic Investment Plan spreads your investment over different times, there is still a risk of holding onto an asset that is losing value. A Lump Sum will expose you to more risk at the beginning. However if prices are rising over the period you may get better returns as you have invested as a whole.

There is no reliable way to know if prices will rise or fall straight after a particular date. Trying to find the perfect time to invest can turn into a guessing game. You may also have no money left in your household if you invest all your money at once. A correct analysis should take into consideration how liquid your money is, how far ahead you are looking and what you are investing for.

## Example

Let’s look at an example with ₹60,000. One option would be to invest the whole amount at the beginning. The other option would be to invest ₹10,000 each month for 6 months. Let’s say that the unit price changes over this period (this is not actual market data and will not predict future performances).

If the prices are rising then the Lump Sum investor will have more money invested at the beginning of the period. The Systematic Investor will invest some of their cash later therefore some units will be bought at a higher price. This means that the Lump Sum may have a higher value at the end. If the prices fall at the beginning and then rise, the Systematic Investor will buy more units at the lower price. The Lump Sum will lose value at the beginning. Depending on the exact prices and when they occur, the Systematic may come out on top or the other way round. If the prices are falling and stay low then both investors could lose money. Simply spreading the investments won’t help if the investment is consistently poor.

To fairly compare you need to ensure you are investing into the same scheme, with the same end date. All of the money should be invested into the scheme, with any relevant costs. The Systematic Investment Plan will have some cash outside of the scheme. You also need to consider that the final percentage may be misleading as you aren’t comparing equal amounts.

## Benefits of Systematic Investment Plans

If you are a salaried individual then investing regularly can mirror the income you receive each month. You may not have much cash to invest so using a Systematic Investment Plan can help keep things simple. You may feel less pressured to invest at a particular date as you will be investing regularly. You can also review your investment if your income or essential costs change, as long as you stick to your scheme and mandate rules.

Although this can help keep things simple, this doesn’t mean it is the right option. If you are investing into a scheme with more volatility than you can handle then it is a risky investment. Automated payments can also continue even if your original reason for investing has changed without reviewing. Just like with a one off investment, you should research the product.

## Benefits of Lump Sums

A Lump Sum may be the best option for a one off investment such as a matured deposit. You should have already considered taxation, debt, short term costs and emergency savings. You will invest all your money at once meaning you are achieving the desired split. It can also be easier to track.

However the downside to investing at one point is that you are exposed to more risk. If the price drops suddenly you may not be happy with this, especially if you weren’t expecting the market to be volatile in the short term. You shouldn’t invest using a Lump Sum because you think the market will rise now that you have the money. If you need the money in a short period of time then it may not be suitable to invest into something linked to the market, whether it is a Lump Sum or multiple investments.

## Cash & Time Horizon

If you have money to pay for rent, medical, upcoming education or a house deposit soon then this shouldn’t be seen as spare cash to invest. Firstly you should know how much of this money is actually available to invest into something linked to the market and when you need it. If you are making regular investments into a SIP then there is an opportunity cost as you are holding onto the cash. This should be considered when comparing.

If you have a long time horizon then you will have more time to ride the volatility. However this doesn’t guarantee that it will recover by your chosen date. The market, scheme and mix of assets will still play a part. If you have a short time horizon then it becomes more important to avoid being forced to sell after a drop. Whatever method you use won’t make up for a mismatch in asset and goal.

## Common Mistakes

Assuming that SIPs are always better because they average the price. This isn’t true. Assuming that Lump Sums are always better because the money is invested for longer. This doesn’t take into consideration the timing risk and how much loss you can tolerate. Comparing a real SIP with a hypothetical Lump Sum (with a different start date) or picking a good time period.

Using past data can show you what has happened over a certain period of time. However this doesn’t tell you which will be better going forward. Charts online may not take into consideration taxation, timing of transactions, cash held between investments or the emotional impact of holding onto your investment after a drop. You should be upfront with your assumptions.

## Questions to Consider

Where is the money coming from? Monthly income or lump sum? Will it be used for a known expense? Do you have any savings available for unexpected events? What are you investing for? How far ahead are you looking? Do you understand the scheme you are investing into? Would you be forced to withdraw or change your plan if there was a sharp drop?

Your answer may vary depending on how the money is split. For example your monthly salary may not need to be invested the same as your bonus which you are saving for your goal. This is a general planning consideration.

## Conclusion

Systematic Investment Plans and Lump Sums are simply two ways to invest money into a mutual fund. A Systematic Investment Plan will spread your investments over different dates and can be a good option for regular income. A Lump Sum will invest your available cash straight away and you will be exposed to more timing risk. The outcome will depend on market performance, timing of your investment, scheme and how much cash you need. A careful comparison should be made with clear assumptions and knowing that both can lose value.

## Investing from Salary

If you are investing from your monthly salary then you may not have the cash available to invest at the beginning. Comparing a monthly SIP investment with a Lump Sum 12 months worth of salary will not be fair as you haven’t actually received the money yet. A fair comparison should be made between available cash now and money expected. This is often a more important consideration than debating abstractly about timing.

If you do have the money available then you need to decide if you want to invest it all now or spread it. By holding some cash aside you are creating a different cash allocation. This should be considered when analysing. You should consider what you are investing for and any constraints you have, rather than using a simplified chart.

## Limitations of Historical Data

You can run a back test to see how each would perform over a historical period. However this will depend on the dates you choose to start and end, scheme, timing of transactions, costs and how you are dealing with the cash. Choosing to start just before a sharp drop may favour the spread investments. Choosing a period with a prolonged rise may favour the Lump Sum. Although these are real historical examples, they won’t tell you what will happen next.

If this data is shown in an article then you should state what period you are looking at and how you have analysed it. A short period may not be representative of a long term goal. The more detailed a chart looks, the more important it is to state what isn’t included.

## The source of the money changes the comparison

A salaried investor who receives money every month is not making the same choice as someone who already has a large amount sitting in a bank account. A monthly SIP can match the timing of salary and make investing more routine. A lump sum may be relevant when a bonus, inheritance or maturity proceeds are already available. In the second case, leaving the whole amount in cash while gradually investing it is also a decision with its own opportunity cost and risks.

Do not create a lump sum just to compare it with an SIP. Borrowing to invest introduces interest costs and repayment risk that are not part of a normal SIP-versus-lump-sum comparison. Likewise, do not invest money needed for an imminent expense simply because a calculator suggests that more time in the market may improve expected outcomes. The goal's date and your ability to tolerate a fall matter as much as the method of investing.
